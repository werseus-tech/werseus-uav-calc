# Lesson 5: How Does a Multirotor Hover? Thrust, Propellers, Disk Loading, and Hover Throttle

### What you will learn
- How multirotor UAVs generate aerodynamic thrust ($T$) and hover based on classical momentum theory.
- The concept of Disk Loading ($DL = T / A$) and why larger propellers consume significantly less power to hover.
- Why the Thrust-to-Weight ratio ($TW$) is targeted at 2.0 or more and how hover throttle is estimated via a non-linear square-root relationship ($\text{Throttle}_{hover} \approx \sqrt{1/TW}$).

### Explanation
Picture paddling a canoe on a calm lake. If you take a wide paddle and push the water backward gently, your canoe glides forward effortlessly. But if you were handed a tiny teaspoon instead, you would have to churn the water very fast, with far more effort, to produce the same push. The spinning propellers of a hovering multirotor interact with the surrounding air in exactly the same way.

A multirotor does not possess lifting wings; it overcomes gravity purely by direct vertical thrust generated by motor-driven propellers. According to Rankine-Froude momentum theory, each spinning propeller disk ($A = \frac{\pi}{4} D_{prop}^2$) captures stagnant ambient air and accelerates it downward through the disk plane at an **Induced Velocity ($v_i$)**. By Newton's third law, accelerating this mass of air downward produces an equal and opposite upward aerodynamic **Thrust ($T$)**. For an $n$-armed multirotor, total weight is split equally among the arms ($T_{arm} = W / n$).

The key parameter governing hover efficiency is **Disk Loading (DL)**, defined as the thrust generated per unit of propeller swept area ($DL = T / A$, expressed in $\text{N/m}^2$). When you increase propeller diameter, the swept disk area expands with the square of diameter, causing disk loading to drop sharply. Low disk loading means pushing a large volume of air downward at low velocity, rather than blasting a small jet of air at hurricane speeds. This reduces the kinetic energy wasted for the same thrust, resulting in better hover efficiency.

Momentum theory defines the ideal, loss-free hover power for a single rotor as:
$$P_{ideal} = T \cdot v_i = \frac{T^{1.5}}{\sqrt{2 \rho A}}$$

In realistic engineering, blade profile friction is accounted for by the Figure of Merit ($FM \approx 0.65$), while brushless motor efficiency ($\eta_{mot} \approx 0.85$) and electronic speed controller efficiency ($\eta_{esc} \approx 0.95$) introduce electrical losses. Consequently, a $1.0\text{ kg}$ quadcopter ($W \approx 9.81\text{ N}$) spinning 5-inch propellers requires approximately $166\text{ W}$ of total electrical power to hover.

Finally, consider the relationship between **Thrust-to-Weight Ratio ($TW$)** and **Hover Throttle**. A general-purpose multirotor design targets a thrust-to-weight ratio of at least $TW \ge 2.0$. This ensures adequate control headroom to counter turbulent wind gusts and execute aggressive climbs. Because aerodynamic thrust scales with the square of rotor rotational speed ($\omega^2$), the throttle signal exhibits a square-root dependence rather than a linear $1/TW$ relationship:
$$\text{Throttle}_{hover} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1}{TW}}$$
At $TW = 2.0$, this simple model gives a hover throttle of roughly $\sqrt{0.5} \approx 71\%$ (real motor-propeller curves typically give 50-70%). If $TW$ drops below 1.6, the hover throttle rises dangerously close to maximum output, leaving little authority to arrest rapid descents or recover from vortex ring state.

### Mini experiment
1. **Propeller Upsizing:** In the software, increase the propeller diameter from 5 inches (127 mm) to 6 inches (152.4 mm). Observe that the swept disk area expands, reducing Disk Loading ($DL$) from $193.5\text{ N/m}^2$ down to $134.4\text{ N/m}^2$, which lowers total electrical hover power by approximately 15-17% ($P \propto 1/\sqrt{A}$).
2. **Insufficient Thrust Margin:** Keep the 5-inch propellers but simulate a heavy sensor payload by increasing the UAV mass from $1.0\text{ kg}$ to $1.5\text{ kg}$. Observe that the $TW$ ratio drops to $1.33$, hover throttle surges above 80%, and the program flags warnings: **"Thrust-to-weight ratio low (T/W < 1.6)"** and **"Hover throttle percentage high (> %60)"**.

### Common misconceptions
1. **"Hover throttle is linearly $1/TW$, so $TW=2$ means exactly 50% throttle":** Propeller thrust scales with the square of motor RPM. Therefore, the throttle-thrust relation is non-linear; the simple model uses a square-root curve ($\text{Throttle} \approx \sqrt{1/TW} \approx 71\%$).
2. **"Using larger propellers increases disk loading":** Exactly the opposite. Disk loading is thrust divided by area ($DL = T / A$). Because disk area grows quadratically with diameter, larger propellers LOWER the disk loading, thereby improving hover efficiency.
3. **"A $TW$ ratio of 1.1 is plenty because thrust exceeds weight":** A multirotor with $TW = 1.1$ can barely lift off in a windless room. In real outdoor conditions, it will lose altitude during maneuvers, fail to arrest a fast descent, and may lose control. A widely used design guideline for general-purpose multirotors is $TW \ge 2.0$.

### Mini quiz
**1. A 4-motor quadcopter has a takeoff mass of $1.0\text{ kg}$ and each motor produces a maximum static thrust of $5.0\text{ N}$. What is the Thrust-to-Weight ratio ($TW$)? ($g \approx 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$)**
A) 1.02
B) 2.04
C) 4.00
D) 0.51
*Correct Answer:* B
*Explanation:* Total maximum thrust from 4 motors is $T_{max,tot} = 4 \times 5.0\text{ N} = 20.0\text{ N}$. The vehicle weight is $W = m \cdot g = 1.0 \times 9.81 = 9.81\text{ N}$. The thrust-to-weight ratio is $TW = 20.0 / 9.81 \approx 2.04$.

**2. When propeller diameter is increased on a multirotor, how do Disk Loading ($DL$) and hover power efficiency change?**
A) Disk loading increases, power efficiency decreases
B) Disk loading decreases, power efficiency increases (less power required to hover)
C) Disk loading and power efficiency remain unchanged
D) Motor power drops to zero
*Correct Answer:* B
*Explanation:* Propeller swept area ($A = \frac{\pi}{4} D^2$) grows quadratically with diameter, lowering disk loading ($DL = T/A$). Lower disk loading reduces induced air velocity, slashing kinetic losses and boosting hover efficiency.

**3. What is the recommended minimum Thrust-to-Weight ratio ($TW$) for a general-purpose multirotor platform?**
A) At least 0.8
B) At least 1.1
C) At least 2.0
D) At least 10.0
*Correct Answer:* C
*Explanation:* A common design guideline (e.g., Quan 2017) targets $TW \ge 2.0$ for general-purpose multirotors, leaving power headroom to resist wind, maneuver, and recover from fast descents.
