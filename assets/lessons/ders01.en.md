# Lesson 1: How Does an Airplane Fly? Lift, Angle of Attack, and Airspeed

### What you will learn
- How lift ($L$) balances weight ($W$) to keep a fixed-wing UAV in steady level flight.
- How angle of attack ($\alpha$) increases the lift coefficient ($C_L$) and why exceeding the limit leads to aerodynamic stall.
- The quadratic effect of airspeed ($V$) on lift and how the required angle of attack adjusts across different flight speeds.

### Explanation
Imagine sticking your hand out the window of a moving car. If you keep your palm completely parallel to the ground, your hand slices through the air effortlessly. But the moment you tilt your fingertips upward, the oncoming airflow forcefully deflects underneath and pushes your hand upward. This very phenomenon is the foundation of how a fixed-wing aircraft stays airborne.

An aircraft wing features a specialized aerodynamic cross-section known as an airfoil. As the airplane moves forward, the air flowing over and under the wing is directed downward. In accordance with Newton's third law of action-reaction and fluid pressure differentials, deflecting the air downward causes the air to exert an equal and opposite upward reaction force on the wing. This upward force is called **Lift ($L$)**. In steady, unaccelerated level flight, this lift force must exactly balance the gravitational pull of the aircraft, which is its **Weight ($W$)**, meaning $L = W$.

The angle between the oncoming relative airflow and the wing chord line is called the **Angle of Attack ($\alpha$)**. Much like your tilted hand out the car window, increasing the angle of attack linearly increases the wing's lift coefficient ($C_L$) up to a specific limit. However, if you tilt the wing too far (typically beyond $12^\circ$ to $15^\circ$), the airflow can no longer follow the curved upper surface. The flow separates, resulting in a sudden, drastic drop in lift. This dangerous aerodynamic condition is known as a **stall**.

The single most influential factor governing lift is **Airspeed ($V$)**. The dynamic pressure exerted by the oncoming air grows with the square of the speed ($V^2$). Doubling the airspeed (at the same angle of attack) quadruples the dynamic pressure and the lift generated by the wing. Consequently, when an aircraft cruises at high speed, it needs only a small angle of attack to support its weight. Conversely, at low speeds during takeoff or landing, it must fly at a significantly higher angle of attack to stay aloft.

These physical relationships are encapsulated in the fundamental aerodynamic lift equation:
$$L = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_L$$

Here, $\rho$ represents the air density (approximately $1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$ at mean sea level), $V$ is the true airspeed ($\text{m/s}$), $S$ is the wing planform area ($\text{m}^2$), and $C_L$ is the non-dimensional lift coefficient determined by the angle of attack and airfoil camber. For example, a $2.0\text{ kg}$ UAV ($W \approx 19.6\text{ N}$) with a wing area of $0.32\text{ m}^2$ flying at a cruise speed of $18\text{ m/s}$ requires a modest lift coefficient of only $C_L \approx 0.31$ to maintain level flight.

### Mini experiment
1. **Airspeed Variation:** In the program, reduce the cruise airspeed from $18\text{ m/s}$ to $12\text{ m/s}$. Observe that dynamic pressure drops by more than half, forcing the required level-flight $C_L$ to rise from $0.31$ to approximately $0.70$. Notice that the airplane needs a higher angle of attack to fly slower.
2. **Mass Increase:** Keep the airspeed fixed at $18\text{ m/s}$ and increase the UAV mass from $2.0\text{ kg}$ to $2.5\text{ kg}$. As weight increases, observe that the required $C_L$ climbs from $0.31$ to approximately $0.39$, showing that the airplane must fly at a higher angle of attack to carry the heavier weight at the same speed.

### Common misconceptions
1. **"The motor directly lifts the airplane upward":** In a fixed-wing aircraft, the motor provides forward thrust only. The lift force overcoming gravity is produced strictly by the wings as airflow passes over them due to forward velocity.
2. **"Angle of attack is the angle of the aircraft nose relative to the horizon":** Angle of attack ($\alpha$) is defined relative to the oncoming relative wind vector, not the artificial horizon. If an aircraft is descending rapidly while its nose points upward, the angle of attack can be dangerously high.
3. **"Increasing the angle of attack always produces more lift":** Lift increases with angle of attack only up to the critical angle ($\alpha_{crit}$, typically $12^\circ - 15^\circ$). Beyond this critical threshold, airflow separates from the upper wing surface, causing an aerodynamic stall and loss of altitude.

### Mini quiz
**1. For a $2.0\text{ kg}$ fixed-wing UAV flying in steady, unaccelerated level flight, what must the lift force ($L$) be?**
A) $0\text{ N}$
B) Approximately $19.6\text{ N}$
C) At least $50\text{ N}$
D) Equal to the maximum motor thrust
*Correct Answer:* B
*Explanation:* In steady level flight, vertical forces must be balanced ($L = W$). The gravitational weight of a $2.0\text{ kg}$ mass is $W = m \cdot g = 2.0 \times 9.81 \approx 19.6\text{ N}$, so the wing must produce exactly $19.6\text{ N}$ of lift.

**2. If all other flight parameters remain constant and airspeed is doubled, how does the lift force change?**
A) It is halved
B) It doubles
C) It quadruples
D) It remains unchanged
*Correct Answer:* C
*Explanation:* In the lift equation ($L = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S C_L$), velocity is squared ($V^2$). Doubling the velocity ($2^2 = 4$) quadruples both dynamic pressure and the generated lift force.

**3. What happens if the wing's angle of attack is increased beyond the critical angle ($\alpha_{crit}$)?**
A) The aircraft breaks the speed of sound
B) Airflow separates from the wing surface, causing an aerodynamic stall
C) Lift increases infinitely
D) The propulsion motor automatically stops
*Correct Answer:* B
*Explanation:* Exceeding the critical angle of attack causes boundary layer flow separation over the upper surface of the wing. This results in a sudden loss of lift and a sharp increase in drag, known as an aerodynamic stall.
